"There's no way to write fiction and guarantee your readers will understand it correctly," Chiang says. "There is no way you will ever be able to write something so straightforward that Elon Musk will understand it the way that you intend it." People in tech seem determined to view speculative fiction through the lens of their own certainties, and "you cannot adjust for readers who are arbitrarily bad at reading."

There are alternatives, Chiang notes. "You can write fiction which is all about how LLMs [large language models] are crap," he says. "Or you could write stories about an A.I. which proposes solutions to climate change but they are all terrible."

Another alternative is to "write about sentient machines in such a different context that it doesn't seem like you're writing about our immediate future." You could put a story in the distant future, an alternate past, or a secondary world like China Miéville's Bas-lag. You could also write in more of a Golden Age science fiction mode, in which you explain carefully the scientific basis of the A.I. in your world, making it clear that you're not writing about LLMs.

Any of those approaches might make your story less likely to be received as a commentary on our current situation, Chiang notes.
